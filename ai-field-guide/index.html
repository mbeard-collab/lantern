Part 00

The 60-second version

Ten habits. Everything after this is just the reasoning behind them.

  1. One thread, one deliverable. New task, new chat. This single habit fixes your output quality and your usage burn at the same time.
  2. Say why, not just what. Who reads this, what decision it drives, what happens if it's wrong. That's the cheapest quality upgrade available.
  3. Source first, question last. Paste the transcript, the doc, the data, then ask. Most people do it backwards.
  4. Don't ask it to remember. Give it the memory. Anything it needs to know every time lives in a Project, not in your chat history.
  5. Never let it invent a number. If a figure is going to a customer, it comes from Spark, Salesforce, or a spreadsheet, not from the model's head.
  6. Pick the right tool before you type. Gemini for things living in Google. Claude for thinking, writing, and multi-step work. Spark for procurement data. See the Router.
  7. Kill the chat when it goes sideways. A bad answer poisons everything after it. Restart, don't argue.
  8. Getting it to agree with you proves nothing. It will agree with almost anything if you push. Test the conclusion in a fresh chat.
  9. Turn off what you're not using. Web search, extra connectors, Chrome. Every one of them is charged on every message whether you use it or not.
  10. Read it before you send it. Your name is on it. "The AI wrote it" is not a thing you get to say to a customer.

The one-sentence test, from Anthropic's own docs: show your prompt to a colleague who knows nothing about the task and ask them to follow it. If they'd be confused, Claude will be too.

The Problem Part 01

The Router

We pay for Claude and we pay for Google Workspace with Gemini in it. Most people default to whichever tab is already open, which is why you see someone fighting Claude for twenty minutes to reformat a spreadsheet that Gemini would have done in place.

These tools are not competitors. They're different layers. Gemini is the ambient layer inside the tools you already live in: Gmail, Docs, Sheets, Drive. Claude is the deep-work layer: research, analysis, judgment, anything with more than three steps. Spark's MCP is the data layer. Pick before you type.

The Problem Part 02

The Brief

The average prompt people type is under nine words. The average prompt that works is about twenty-one. That gap is the whole game, and it isn't about typing more, it's about typing the four things the model can't guess.

Half the advice you've read about prompting is from 2023 and is now actively unhelpful. You don't need to tell it it's a "world-class expert." You don't need to say "think step by step". The current models already do. What you need is a brief, the same one you'd give a sharp new hire who starts today and knows nothing about us.

The Move

Four parts. Then the why.

01 · Role

One sentence, and only if it's real. "You're writing as an AE to a county procurement director." Not a paragraph of fake credentials.

02 · Task

A verb and an object. Draft. Compare. Rank. Extract. Summarize into. If order matters, number the steps.

03 · Context

The account, the history, the constraint, the source material. This is the part people skip and it's the part that matters most.

04 · Format

What you want back. Length, structure, tone. "Six bullets" beats "be concise."

Then add the why. Who reads this, what decision it drives, what happens if it's wrong. Anthropic calls this "context and motivation" and it is the highest-leverage cheap move in the whole practice. "This goes to a CFO who already turned us down once" changes the output more than any amount of persona theatre.

Same request, two ways

Flip between them.

Under nine words. Gets you a generic template.
Write a follow-up email to this prospect.

You're writing as me, an AE at GovSpend, which sells government procurement intelligence. Draft a follow-up email to a county purchasing director I demoed to last Thursday. Context (transcript pasted below): she liked the bid alerts, was lukewarm on the contacts data, and said budget is the real blocker until their FY starts in October. Address it without naming the competitor. --- [paste transcript here]

Four moves that reliably work

Show, don't describe

Paste one real email that landed instead of describing the tone you want. One good example beats three paragraphs of adjectives. Two is usually plenty. The models pay very close attention to examples now, so a sloppy example teaches sloppy output.

Say what to do, not what to avoid

"Write in flowing paragraphs" works. "Don't use bullet points" works less well. Negative instructions make the model think about the thing you don't want.

Give it permission to not know

Add: "If you're not sure, say so rather than guessing." It sounds too simple to matter. It measurably cuts made-up answers, because the default incentive is to guess.

Ask for the artifact, not the essay

"Give me the five bullets" instead of accepting 1,200 words you'll delete. Long outputs cost you twice: once to generate, and again on every message after, because the whole thread gets re-read each turn.

Two habits to retire. Stop writing "IMPORTANT: YOU MUST" in caps. Current models over-react to it and get worse, not better. And stop saying please to make it try harder; it's a brief, not a favor. Be direct.

The Problem Part 03

The Thread

Nobody tells you this and it explains almost every complaint people have: your entire conversation is re-sent to the model on every single message. Not summarized. Re-sent. In full.

The 40-page PDF you attached in message three is being re-read on message thirty. So is every dead end, every wrong turn, every "no, not like that." That's why quality drops as a chat gets long, and it's why you hit your limit on a Tuesday afternoon.

It gets worse than "slower." Research across eighteen models found that performance doesn't just plateau with long context. It gets unreliable. Add four irrelevant distractors to a prompt and accuracy drops substantially. Your 60-message thread is mostly distractors.

The Move

Know the tells

Start a new chat the moment you see any of these. Not "after I finish this one thing". Right then. Every message after a bad one inherits the bad one.

It repeats itself

Or gives you a variation of the answer it just gave.

It forgets your rules

You said no bullets four times and it's using bullets.

It claims it did something it didn't

"I've updated that section". And it hasn't.

It goes soft on you

It stops pushing back and starts agreeing with everything. This one's the sneakiest.

You're on attempt five

Four failed tries means the brief was wrong, not the answer. Rewrite the brief somewhere clean.

You changed subjects

New deliverable, new thread. Always. No exceptions worth arguing about.

The carry-forward move

Don't paste your old chat into the new one. You'd be importing the exact junk you're trying to escape. Ask for a briefing instead, then start clean with just that.

Summarize everything we established in this conversation that a fresh assistant would need in order to continue: decisions made, constraints, facts about the account, tone preferences, and open questions. Write it as a briefing document, not a recap of our conversation.

Paste that as the first message of the new chat. Re-attach only the source files you still need. You've kept the signal and dropped forty turns of noise.

Monday

Put the permanent stuff somewhere permanent

If you're re-explaining GovSpend, your territory, or your ICP at the top of every chat, that's a Project, not a paragraph. Files you upload to a Project get cached. You stop paying full price to re-read them, and you stop paying the long-thread quality tax at the same time.

Put in a Project
  • Your ICP and territory definition
  • Objection library / battlecards
  • Product one-pagers and pricing structure
  • Three emails of yours that landed, as tone samples
  • Your call-notes template
Keep out of a Project
  • Anything for one account only
  • Anything that changes weekly
  • Raw exports you'll use once
  • Anything under Rule 1 in Part 06
Order matters

For anything long (a transcript, a contract, an export), put the material first and your question last. Anthropic measured up to a 30% quality improvement from that ordering alone on complex multi-document inputs. Most people do it backwards out of habit.

The Problem Part 04

The Burn

"I ran out again." Here's the part nobody explained: our seats are Team Standard, and every surface (chat, Cowork, Chrome, Claude Code) draws from one shared pool, per person, on a rolling window. There's no team pot to raid. When you're out, you're out until it resets.

The good news is that burn is almost entirely a function of habits, not volume. The people who never hit the wall aren't using it less. They're using it differently.

The Move

What actually eats your usage

Ranked. Anthropic's own ordering, translated.

Cowork sessions
Agentic loops generate enormous intermediate output. Anthropic warns about this explicitly. Worth it for a real multi-step job; catastrophic for "what's a good subject line."
Claude in Chrome
Every page it reads lands in context and stays there. It is the fastest way to empty a week's usage in an afternoon.
Long threads
Quadratic, not linear. Message 30 pays for messages 1 through 29. This is the one most people are guilty of.
Screenshots & PDFs
A 40-page PDF runs 60,000–120,000 tokens because every page is processed as text and as an image. A screenshot of a table costs ~10x what the pasted text costs.
Connectors left switched on
Every enabled connector adds its tool definitions to every message, used or not. Turn off what this task doesn't need.
Web search on by default
Fine when you need current facts. Pure waste when the answer is in the doc you attached.
Extended thinking / high effort
Real value on analysis and comparison. Dead weight on drafting and rewriting.
Plain chat
Drafting, summarizing, Q&A. This is the cheap stuff. Most of your work should live here.
Monday

Seven habits that buy back a day

01

Batch your questions

Five related questions in one message costs a fraction of five messages. Plan the message before you type it.

02

Extract before you attach

Need three pages of a 60-page deck? Paste the three pages. Don't make it read the other 57 on every turn.

03

Paste text, never a screenshot

If the text exists as text, use it as text. A 1000×1000 screenshot costs about 1,300 tokens. The same content pasted costs a couple hundred.

04

Projects instead of re-pasting

Project files are cached. Re-uploading the same one-pager into twelve chats is twelve full charges for the same document.

05

Match the model to the job

The heavy model can use several times the tokens of the fast one for the same task. Reformatting and tone edits don't need the heavy one.

06

Stop at four failures

The fifth "no, more like this" costs more than a clean restart and produces worse output. Rewrite the brief.

07

Check your meter

Settings → Usage. Look at it once a week for a month. You'll self-correct faster from seeing your own number than from anything on this page.

Bonus

Know the escape hatch

Gemini is included in your Workspace seat and has its own separate quota. Out of Claude at 3pm on a Thursday? Drafting and summarizing move over to Gemini fine.

The Problem Part 05

The Check

Ask a model to summarize a document you gave it and it's wrong maybe two to five percent of the time. Ask it a specific fact from memory (a prospect's headcount, last year's contract value, who runs procurement there), and on obscure questions it's closer to a coin flip.

That gap is the whole lesson. Grounded in a source you provided: reliable. Pulled from memory: not. Everything below is just discipline about which one you're doing.

And it won't warn you. One benchmark found the models that admit uncertainty most often are roughly three times more accurate when they do answer. The confident ones are confident because guessing scores better than abstaining, not because they know.

Four prompts that do the checking for you

The last one only works in a new chat. That's the point of it.

Grounding lock: before you start
Answer using only the documents I've attached. If the answer isn't in them, say "Not in the source material." Do not fill gaps from general knowledge.
Audit pass: after it drafts
For each factual claim you just made, quote the exact sentence from the source that supports it. If you can't find one, delete the claim and tell me what you removed.
Confidence tagging: for research
Mark every claim as [VERIFIED: with quote], [INFERRED: with your reasoning], or [UNSURE]. I'm only going to use the VERIFIED ones externally.
Adversarial pass: paste into a NEW chat
Here's a draft and the source material it was built from. List every factual claim in the draft that the source doesn't actually support. Be harsh. I'd rather be embarrassed by you than by a customer.

A citation is not verification. Models will happily attach a real-looking source to a claim they already made. A citation only counts if it includes a quote you can find, or a link you actually opened. Numbers, names, dates and dollar figures get clicked. Prose framing doesn't.

Part 06

The Rules

Everything else in this guide is a suggestion: try it, adapt it, tell us what works better. This part isn't. It's short on purpose, because a policy nobody can recite isn't a policy.

Never, no exceptions
  • Credentials, API keys, passwords, tokens
  • Customer PII: SSNs, bank details, payment cards, home addresses
  • Anything covered by an NDA that isn't ours to share
  • Unreleased financials, board material, M&A or deal-diligence material
  • Anything under legal hold or attorney-client privilege
  • Our security architecture, DR plans, or pen-test results
  • Work data in a personal AI account. Company tenant only.
Ask first
  • A customer's internal documents they sent us in confidence
  • Contract language or redlines before Legal has seen them
  • Anything with a named individual's employment or performance details
  • Any new AI tool you want to expense. It goes through the tech-purchase review, same as any other vendor
  • Any customer-facing automation that sends something without a human reading it first
Go ahead
  • GovSpend procurement data. It's public record, that's the product
  • Public bids, contracts, spend, agency contacts
  • Your own call notes and transcripts
  • Our marketing material, published pricing, public collateral
  • Your drafts, your emails, your analysis
  • Anything you'd be comfortable reading aloud on a customer call

Two more, and then we're done

Security questions go to the queue, not to Claude. If a prospect or customer asks anything about our security posture, certifications, architecture, or data handling. You don't answer it, and you don't have an AI answer it. It goes in as a Salesforce case and routes to the DevSecOps queue. We are mid-SOC 2 and mid-ISO; a well-meaning wrong answer in an email becomes a finding. This is the single highest-risk thing a commercial person can do with an AI tool here.

Turn off the thumbs. Our Team plan doesn't train on our conversations, but clicking thumbs-up or thumbs-down explicitly opts that specific conversation in. Don't rate conversations that contain anything you wouldn't publish.

Why we're not being stricter than this

Most of what we work with is public procurement data. That's genuinely low risk and we'd rather you use these tools aggressively than tiptoe. The list above is short because it's the part that actually matters: credentials, other people's confidential information, and anything that could commit GovSpend to a security claim we haven't verified.

If you're unsure, the test is simple: would you be comfortable if this showed up in a customer's inbox with your name on it? If yes, go. If no, ask.

Part 08

Fifteen things to stop doing

Every one of these is documented in the research, and every one of these happens here. No judgment. Most of them are the intuitive thing to do. They're just wrong.

01Typing one line and hoping

"Write an email to this prospect." Nine words or fewer, no context, no audience, no stakes. You get back the average of every follow-up email on the internet, which is exactly what it sounds like.

FixRole, task, context, format, then say why it matters and who reads it. Twenty-one words is the benchmark, and context is most of them.
02Running everything in one giant thread

One chat, all day, every topic. It's the single most common habit here and it's costing you twice: quality degrades as the thread fills with irrelevant history, and every message pays for every message before it.

FixOne thread, one deliverable. Carry context forward with a briefing summary, not by pasting the old chat.
03Taking the first output

The first response is a draft sampled from the most generic version of the answer. It is never the best one available and it's usually not close.

FixOne round, always: "What's weak about this? Give me a sharper version." Or ask for three variants and pick.
04Editing the output instead of rewriting the brief

You spend fifteen minutes fixing an email. You've now absorbed all of its structural choices and you're slower than if you'd written it yourself.

FixIf you're changing more than about a third of it, the brief was wrong. Rewrite the brief. Bonus: paste your edited version back in as the example of what good looks like.
05Asking it to do math

It predicts text. It doesn't calculate. It'll produce a number that looks right and is off by fifteen percent, with total confidence.

FixMake it use a tool or a spreadsheet, or do the arithmetic yourself and have it narrate the result. Never let a model produce a number that goes in a customer quote.
06Asking for current facts without a source

"How many employees does [company] have?" On specific, obscure, current facts, models from memory are near a coin flip, and the confident tone is identical whether it knows or not.

FixForce a search or a connector call. For anything about procurement, use the GovSpend data. For anything about a person or a company, paste the source.
07Asking it to count things

How many rows, how many words, how many times X appears. It doesn't see characters the way you do, and it will confidently miscount.

FixCtrl-F. A spreadsheet. A tool. Anything but the model's own head.
08Being polite at it

Please, thank you, "you said you'd do this," negotiating, expressing disappointment. It has no memory of a commitment and no capacity for guilt. The politeness is costing you tokens and clarity.

FixTreat it like a brief to a fast, well-read contractor with amnesia. Restate; don't remind. Be direct. That's not rudeness, it's just the format.
09Deciding it "can't do that" after one try

One bad attempt, one conclusion: "it's not good at that." This is the most documented failure mode in the whole literature and it's how people end up with a permanently small idea of what the tool does.

FixRephrase once. Give it one example. Then decide.
10Deciding it "always works" after one try

The opposite error, and more expensive. It worked once, so it becomes the team template, and now it's producing garbage at scale.

FixBefore you share a prompt or build a skill, run it on three real, genuinely different cases.
11Trusting it more as you use it more

Research on 300+ knowledge workers found it directly: the more confident people are in the AI, the less critical thinking they apply. Your checking gets sloppier exactly as your volume goes up.

FixA fixed verification ritual tied to where the output goes, not to how good it felt. See Part 05.
12Arguing until it agrees with you

Push back three or four times and most models fold. You have now generated agreement, not validation, and long threads and personalization both make this worse, not better.

FixTest the conclusion in a fresh chat, stated neutrally or from the opposing side. If it only agrees with you in the thread where you argued for it, you learned nothing.
13The same prompt across forty accounts

You built a great outbound prompt. Now every prospect gets a structurally identical email. Prospects are unusually good at spotting this. It's the exact pattern they've trained themselves to delete.

FixThe template stays constant. The context block (this account, this signal, this person) changes every single time. If two emails could swap accounts unnoticed, start over.
14Using it on the tasks it's worst at

The BCG study found people using AI on tasks outside its range scored nineteen points worse than people with no AI at all. Not slower, worse. The trap is that those tasks feel the same as the ones it's great at.

FixAsk yourself: does answering this correctly require information the model cannot possibly have? If yes, use it to structure your thinking, not to produce the answer.
15Handing it the whole job and walking away

About one in ten people in the BCG study did this: delegate and disengage. They built neither AI skill nor domain skill. The highest performers do the opposite: many of them deliberately work without AI sometimes, specifically to keep their own judgment sharp.

FixStay in the loop. Pause and decide whether this is an AI task before you make it one. That pause is what separates the top group in every study on the subject.
Where this goes next

The tools aren't the constraint anymore. Everybody has them. The gap now is between people who've built habits and people who are still typing one line and hoping.

None of this is a mandate. It's what the people getting good results are actually doing, written down so you don't have to reverse-engineer it. Pick two habits from Part 00 and run them for a week. Start with one-thread-one-deliverable and saying why in your prompts. Those two alone will change your output more than the other eight combined.

If you build something that works (a prompt, a workflow, a way of running a call prep), don't sit on it. Send it over. The ones that hold up on three real cases get packaged as slash commands so everybody gets them.

Questions, corrections, or a play worth stealing: send them to Matt. Security questions go in as a Salesforce case, not to Matt, and definitely not to Claude.

One last thing. Your sense of whether AI is helping you is unreliable. There's a well-known study where experienced professionals were measurably slower using AI while believing they were twenty percent faster. Measure the output. Don't trust the feeling.
